#2. Carrick joins Wilcox under the bus after Manchester United’s latest night in crisis | Max Rushden

You have to credit Manchester United. Your local rivals have turned the Premier League into an almost meaningless farce. We are questioning the validity of anything we’ve seen over the past 20 years; wondering how many goals Erling Haaland will get in the Championship. We are looking at the league table from second position down and wishing we’d invested in inflatable bananas a month ago.

And yet here comes the Old Trafford machine: from players to the captain to the manager to the director of football, to the director of the director of football, to a minority owner who feels the UK is being “colonised by immigrants”, to debt-leveraging majority owners who take vast sums out of the club and are collectively saying hold my Ineos-sponsored high-performance wellness immune boost smoothie.

Yes, it’s time to dust down the Manchester United bin-fire article. Read this and compare and contrast. From 11 August 2022: “Reds rinse and repeat: will we ever break the Manchester United crisis cycle?” There are others.

It is some feat to be more newsworthy than Manchester City, even for an evening. The defeat in Como wasn’t surprising. We’re getting to the stage where the large pieces of furniture Barney Ronay keeps comparing Harry Maguire to are fighting back. @19thCenturyArmoire: “mate i’m a hundred and sixty years old, of course my legs have gone.” @CoffinFridge69: “#BeKind Ronay, appliances have feelings too.”

Everyone knows the problems at Old Trafford lie deeper than the defensive line held by Maguire and Matthijs de Ligt on Wednesday.

Perhaps it was only a matter of time before Bruno Fernandes’s constant on-field complaining spilled off the pitch. An exercise in throwing his manager under the bus, where presumably Michael Carrick found Jason Wilcox, still lying in hiding under the 250 to the Trafford Centre after Lisandro Martínez’s post-match intervention in Madrid.

Carrick did push back on the criticism: “Whatever system you play, whatever tactics you play, if you don’t run, and you don’t run forward, and you don’t run back, you aren’t going to win.” But it’s hard to throw someone under the bus when you’re already under the bus you’re trying to throw people under.

Poor Carrick, who may well be gone by the time I finish this tram ride, never mind by the time you get around to reading this. It was one of those funereal press conferences: Carrick being as earnest as it is possible to be, while journalists take it in turn to sound even more earnest, as if they’re questioning a man who’s just failed to blow up the asteroid coming to destroy the Earth.

At this rate, that meteor will arrive long before United have recaptured past glories. Ineos presumably has a big blame pie chart constantly updating on a PowerPoint. Spin the wheel – who’s to blame today? Maybe the players?

Does anyone genuinely know any more whether any United player is good? I have analysis paralysis when trying to work out whether Fernandes is world class or why Youri Tielemans never lumbered at Aston Villa. How bamboozled will we be when Joshua Zirkzee has 25 goal involvements for Real Sociedad next season?

As for Carrick, while most of us (me included) felt he’d earned the right to manage the side this season, a more ruthless and efficient ownership would have made a different call. It wouldn’t have been popular.

He may turn out to be a world-class manager – he may not. Football decides pretty quickly about these things. But if he is sacked, there is a path to redemption. Daniel Farke has moved beyond “only good in the Championship”. Frank Lampard could reasonably expect an apology from anyone who comments on the game for his achievements at Coventry.

But at this stage it is fair to say Carrick doesn’t have the tactical ability or experience of almost all his competitors in the division.

Above him, is Jason Wilcox good? Perhaps the last time someone yelled at him like Martínez, it was Mike Newell after an overhit cross at Ewood Park in 1991. What will Omar Berrada’s CV look like after the independent panel delivers its punishments?

It is fair to say that barring the odd All or Nothing documentary, I have almost no knowledge of what happens behind the scenes. But £1bn spent by United to create this squad over the past five years, plus the randomness of Rangnick/Ten Hag/Van Nistelrooy/Amorim/Carrick, gives us limited evidence to suggest high performance.

And at the top, what does Jim Ratcliffe care about? Apart from enjoying the tax rates of Monaco while opining about the fallen British empire. The good old days where you could just colonise and get a pat on the back, eh Jim.

And then there’s the Glazers. Even for someone whose brain shuts down as soon as they hear the words “leveraged debt” the fact they have extracted about £1bn out of the club since the takeover in 2005 is an outrage. Perhaps if they had overseen a period of sustained success, but it is the opposite.

So when you add bad (maybe bad?) players, bad (or at least disrespectful) captain, bad (not great yet) manager, bad technical director, bad chief executive, bad minority owner, bad majority owners – apologies if I’ve missed anyone – it is, well, bad.

So what’s the best plan of action now? Did someone say Antonio Conte? For the wind and the rain make it happen. As Paul Watson said on the latest Guardian Football Weekly, he might resign during his unveiling.

Lots of us who lived through the Fergie success can’t help but enjoy a Manchester United crisis, but it’s possible to laugh and at the same time sympathise with powerless United fans under that dripping roof in the East Stand.

Football is so stupid that Conte, or Eddie Howe, or even Carrick could turn this into a good season. Perhaps everyone’s expectations still cling to a Manchester United that do not exist any more. Perhaps the odd trophy, the occasional foray deep into Europe, may be the new normal. But right now, how many Premier League teams are worse than they are? Did someone say a Manchester derby in the Championship next season? I can hear David Prutton’s excitement already.

#3. Elite-level incompetence is Manchester United’s problem – not Michael Carrick | Barney Ronay

At the final whistle at Elland Road the TV cameras cut to a lingering shot of the Manchester United chief executive, Omar Berrada, looking down pensively from the stands, deciding, it is to be hoped, on a little clemency, a re-sheathing of the managerial shotgun.

Here we have the man who hired the man. The man who fires the man. The man who also hired or rehired the previous two men he had to fire. Berrada was poached from Manchester City on the basis of his ability to grow commercial revenue (yes, OK, stop laughing), and appears, like everyone else with any actual reach around here, to exist in a strangely consequence-free bubble.

How do you solve what is, on revenue versus results, the worst-run club in world football? By changing the name of the latest patsy on the touchline, of course. For now a hard-fought point is perhaps enough to stave off that pre-programmed emergency response.

Stand down the cowboy cops. Call off the blue and white saloons. Although with a run of Bournemouth, Chelsea, Aston Villa and Liverpool next up in the league it will no doubt soon be time to go out there and arrest Lee Harvey Oswald again. What’s that you say? OK, well, dig him up again. Re-arrest him. Whatever it takes. Someone has to stop these lone gunmen, these managerial magic bullet merchants from derailing the Manchester United supremacy.

This was a dual-track football match between Leeds and Manchester United, on the face of it a good, even game between two teams of well-matched players. A 1-1 draw was fair in the end, although Leeds played well and probably should have won. But of course they won’t be the story.

Instead this game had already been billed as that familiar thing, a referendum on the future of Michael Carrick. A stay of execution for a week or two. Or another livestreamed firing squad for the Super Sunday cameras.

There has been a predictable sense of low comedy about Carrick’s own transformation in the United dugout this season. It was always going to be the case that his widely praised qualities in victory, blazer, T-shirt, jawline, inscrutable sense of calm, would become his fatal flaws in defeat.

Where’s the passion, the whirling arms, the range of tailored collars? At Elland Road Carrick simply looked drained in the second half as United were pegged back and overrun at times, the beard a little more ragged, the hair unkempt but still sweeping, the look of a brave, doomed tugboat captain resigned now to the approaching tsunami wave on the horizon.

A point leaves United 13th in the league and still struggling to reverse a dreadful start to the season. But it was a good game for two people. First for Carrick, who remains a million miles away from creating the problems afflicting this club, even if you accept that his presence as head coach is one of them.

Michael Carrick did not hire Michael Carrick. Getting rid of Michael Carrick is, by definition, not going to solve the problems that led to his appointment. But it might just help to prolong them. Whatever his ultimate ceiling, it is surely worth keeping him just to try, for once, to do something different.

View image in fullscreen The Manchester United director of football, Jason Wilcox (centre), and chief executive officer Omar Berrada (right) watch on at Elland Road. Photograph: Annabel Lee-Ellis/PA

This was also a good game for Lisandro Martínez, who went off injured at half-time with United 1-0 up, and who was also vindicated in his allegedly controversial (translation: entirely correct) comments on the club hierarchy in midweek.

United’s energetic first-half performance clarified one half of this. The players are committed in support of the manager, at least to the best of their ability. The second half made the other half of the argument, that this remains a mismatched, lopsided, cut-and-paste squad, always likely to be pulled apart by opponents as urgent as this Leeds team, which fully deserves its current seventh place in the table.

The problems are so obvious it is barely worth repeating them. But here goes. The third-richest club in the world played a second half with three of its back four out of position. Harry Maguire, signed under Ole Gunnar Solskjær, remains the best straight defender. But Maguire is also essentially a man-shaped solid wood armoire in a pair of shorts at this stage in his career, always the slowest person on any pitch, which means you will not be able to cover space, play a high line or counterattack without leaving yourself exposed to the counter-counter.

So good luck, guys. And remember Omar, who gave you this whisky bottle and loaded pistol of a squad, will be watching.

This was made painfully clear after half-time as Martínez went off and Noussair Mazraoui came on. Leeds scored an equaliser that felt structurally inevitable. They found space down their right side repeatedly. At times they seemed to be constantly peppering the seats behind the United goal, like a group of kids hammering a ball against a garage door. By the end Leeds had more possession, more shots on target and just looked a happier, better structured entity than the travelling heritage exhibit at the other end.

The key point here is to accept that all of this is entirely logical. This is not a mystery, a haunted house, a place of bad voodoo. Why do we talk about Manchester United so much? For obvious reasons. It’s a massive part of the English football landscape. Mainly, though, because this is a story of mind-bending waste, real-time elite-level incompetence played out on a weekly basis.

Yes, you could sack Michael Carrick. It won’t change much. Let’s destroy Eddie Howe for a bit instead. But the available evidence suggests every single person making executive decisions here is outmatched and outmanoeuvred.

There is a reason Manchester United feels like one of those high street legacy chain shops just before it collapses, piles of Quality Street by the door, a rat in the lingerie department, destined to become a pop-up vape shop run by Albanian money launderers. There’s a reason it feels so badly run. This is because: it is.

View image in fullscreen Lisandro Martínez, who criticised the Manchester United hierarchy in midweek, went off injured at half-time against Leeds. Photograph: Phil Noble/Reuters

We know the Glazer ownership has zero interest in minding the details, that this has been a wonderful acquisition whatever the performance on the pitch, an asset supremely sweated. We know also that Sir Jim Ratcliffe is pub-level incoherent on the details he promised to fix. Remember when he was going to sit down and decide what the “style of football” would be? How’s that working out?

Instead Carrick is left shuffling a first XI acquired by endlessly revolving administrations. Here United had four defenders signed under Erik ten Hag, two Ruben Amorim recruits, two from the mist-shrouded age of Solskjær. No players were recruited under Ralf Rangnick, of course. But then Rangnick wanted to sign Erling Haaland, Luis Díaz, and Julian Álvarez, which all sounds far too sensible.

An organisation can be built with many hands. But not when so many of those hands appear to be so far from best in class it’s hard to work out which class they’re supposed to be attending. John Murtough, the former Manchester United football director, is currently director of global development at Atalanta, who have a 24,000-seat stadium and almost zero global presence. Meanwhile Berrada, the axeman, seems entirely invulnerable from the axe himself, despite the fact his job seems to orbit around resolving the consequences of his own mistakes.

United probably will sack their current manager before the year is out. This is the way this machine has to operate, always looking to buy itself, and those in the executive seats, a little more time on the payroll, an entity that has been hollowed out and allowed to rust, run on buzzwords and brown paper and string. Meanwhile, over to you Michael. We’ll see you next week, and maybe even the week after that, for more of exactly the same.

#4. Martínez has drawn back the curtain on Manchester United’s ruinous ownership | Jonathan Liew

Heartbreaking: the worst defender you know just made a great point. And no, nobody would describe Lisandro Martínez as the ideal person to scrutinise Manchester United’s indifferent start to the season, given that his high-profile defensive errors against Fulham and Tottenham are partly responsible for it. “We’re all trying to find the guy who did this,” Martínez burbles on Amazon Prime after United’s 2-0 defeat by Atlético Madrid on Tuesday night, dressed in a hot dog costume and throwing his arms out in mock innocence.

For those with no interest in engaging with what Martínez actually had to say, this alone will be sufficient grounds to ignore him. Already you can sense the squeamishness of former players for whom the violation of an omertà is the real cardinal sin here, a phantom limb that still tingles years after they last stepped into a dressing room. “Disrespectful,” reckoned Wayne Rooney. “I don’t feel like he’s got the right,” agreed Ashley Young. “These things don’t end well, man,” Rio Ferdinand observed on his podcast.

But traducing the messenger will always be easier than hearing the message, even if Martínez’s views are widely shared within the United dressing room. If the fallout from the Manchester City legal case has demonstrated anything, it is English football’s ingrained reluctance to scrutinise the owners and executives who hold more influence over the sport than anybody else. Men in boots, not men in suits. Hey, watch the ball. It’s going to move!

All the same, it’s worth parsing exactly what Martínez was complaining about, because it’s a good deal more complex than it may appear on first viewing. Player calls out board – and specifically “technical director” Jason Wilcox – for failing to invest sufficiently in the squad over the summer. This was a summer in which United brought in three midfielders (Andrey Santos, Youri Tielemans, Carlos Baleba), a reserve goalkeeper (Karl Darlow), nobody up front and nobody in defence. So far, so simple.

But let’s begin to unpick the threads. Why did United not buy a defender in the summer, despite pursuing deals for Lewis Hall and Myles Lewis-Skelly? Because they were unable to sell a defender. There was no interest in Diogo Dalot or Noussair Mazraoui, nor in Harry Maguire, nor in Luke Shaw or Martínez, whose contracts are up next summer in any case. This is the product of recruitment decisions made and deals signed years ago. But why do United, one of the world’s most lucrative clubs, need to sell to buy in the first place?

Perhaps for the same reason they baulked at the asking price for Elliot Anderson and decided to sign the inconsistent Santos and the adequate but overpromoted Tielemans instead: this is a club in austerity measures, whose part-owner Jim Ratcliffe has been furiously cutting costs and raising ticket prices, determined to curb the years of excess that preceded him, where mediocre players were signed on ridiculous money, where failing coaches were paid to leave. But why would United, one of the most commercially successful clubs in footballing history, ever need to cut costs?

Perhaps as a result of the more than £1bn drained out of the club by the Glazers in their 21 years of ownership, not just in dividends but share sales and interest payments on the debt they undertook to buy the club, while neglecting the maintenance of Old Trafford and the training ground at Carrington. Whether he knows it or not, when Martínez is criticising the lack of investment he is in effect holding the Glazers accountable for more than two decades of avarice and mismanagement.

All of which lends a certain significance to Martínez’s intervention that goes beyond the churn of the weekly news cycle. Has any serving United player spoken out so frankly about the ruins of the Glazer ownership? Ole Gunnar Solskjær made a minor stand in early 2005, before they had arrived at the club. Cristiano Ronaldo threw some hand grenades over his shoulder a few days before he left for the second time in 2022. Countless others waited for the safety of retirement, when their disgruntlement could be monetised for personal gain, rather than used to stigmatise them, as Martínez will doubtless be stigmatised in the coming days.

View image in fullscreen A Manchester United supporter shows off a jumper protesting against the club’s owners and executives. Photograph: Robbie Jay Barratt/AMA/Getty Images

Does any of this help United to win games in the short term? Almost certainly not. Martínez attempted to clarify his comments in a social media post on Wednesday night. He may still be dispatched to the bomb squad anyway, for the crime of pointing out that the emperor secured his throne almost entirely through the use of payment-in-kind loans.

Inevitably Michael Carrick too will be gone before long, away to the great Overlap podcast in the sky. A few months ago I wrote hopefully that Carrick might be the ideal man to cut through the noise and nonsense. I was wrong. The noise has beaten him; the nonsense always wins; any accumulated credit from last season has long since evaporated.

Of course it’s not Carrick’s fault that United haven’t signed a new defender in almost two years, that Bruno Fernandes is so clearly playing through discomfort, that Joshua Zirkzee is still somehow hanging around the place, that the ownership decided to embark on a purge of season-ticket holders in the summer, generating a huge wave of ill-feeling and apathy that can be felt at Old Trafford on matchdays: an entire fanbase trying to work out whether they actually still care about this.

But accountability is for the little people, and far easier to replace a coach on a modest 20-month contract than to oust Ineos or the Glazers, to replace Wilcox or Omar Berrada, even if we now know they partly built their reputations on the success of a club that has been found to have serially cheated. Bumper returns for the owners, price hikes for the fans, corners cut on the pitch; this is the story of the modern Manchester United, which by no coincidence is also the story of modern football.

#5. Football Daily | Post-Ferguson worn-out horror show continues at Manchester United

NIGHTMARE ON LAKE COMO

We’re in the midst of what the kids call “spooky season” – or, for our fellow fusty old-timers out there, “autumn”. If you’ve been cosying up with a few horror flicks this month, you’ll know nothing good can come from hanging around a lake at this time of year. And so it proved for Manchester United, who endured an early Halloween nightmare on the banks of Lake Como that may have driven a stake through the Michael Carrick era. Hailed as the interim anti-Amorim not so long ago, Carrick has overseen two wins this season, against Ipswich and Sabah Baku. Como were in Serie D when Carrick won Big Cup with United as a player in 2008 – now, 18 years on, he watched the Italian upstarts dance, twirl and shimmy through the set of training cones he had sent out to represent his storied club.

In the dugout, Carrick appeared frozen by fear. If there had been a sofa perched on the touchline, he may well have crouched behind it to avoid the series of jump-scares served up by his players. At the end, he walked over to the three United fans who had stuck around and held up his hands, looking like a cross between Ole Gunnar Solskjær and a doomed Squid Game contestant. But somehow, things were about to get worse. A week after Lisandro Martínez had taken aim at (or, in truth, just below) United’s hapless hierarchy, Bruno Fernandes decided to swing a little lower. Asked about his side’s tactics, Fernandes said it was “on the manager and staff to see”, before offering a surprisingly detailed dissection of Carrick’s pressing techniques. Now 32, perhaps Fernandes is eyeing the lucrative podcast market, where former United players are busy making hay by stating the obvious. Perhaps he’s just full of it. Carrick fired back with a maxim that works for horror-movie protagonists and lazy footballers alike: “If you don’t run, you ain’t going to win.”

In truth, Manchester United’s entire post-Ferguson era feels like a worn-out horror franchise, a repetitive cycle of diminishing returns that has churned through managerial victims, while still finding new ways of scraping the barrel. United fans might have dared to dream that City’s guilty verdict marked the end of the nightmare; instead, a new team of FFP-baiting waifs in shiny blue shirts have appeared, also led by a crafty Catalan. Carrick might not make it past United’s trip to Stamford Bridge on Halloween, but could Cesc Fàbregas replace him? Asked before the game, the Como head coach snapped back: “I don’t think that shows enough respect to a great footballer and manager like Michael Carrick, at the helm of a wonderful Manchester United side.” Tone it down a bit, Cesc, it’s giving sarcastic. Fabregas is on the bookies’ speculative longlist to replace Carrick, but it’s hard to fathom why he would take the job. At Como, he has total oversight of a talented team, a financial stake in the club and all the perks of Italian lakeside living. It’s a lot to give up just to be pushed around by Jason Wilcox.

Big Website reports that Frightfest tropes Julian Nagelsmann (hipster lost in woods) and Unai Emery (vampire) are the leading candidates. Our personal pick, purely for content reasons, would be Antonio Conte, who would surely combust with rage within a week. Ultimately, will it matter? We all know the big reveal by now: the real villains at Old Trafford are in the boardroom, hollowing out the mistreated mega-club like an overripe pumpkin. Much like reading a tea-timely email packed with too-early Halloween gags, bringing United back to relevance is a tortuous process that nobody has the strength to complete. For now, the nightmare continues.

#8. Cesc Fabregas explains why Como invoked the mercy rule against Manchester United

Joshua Zirkzee's stoppage-time consolation goal did nothing for Manchester United head coach Michael Carrick's hopes of holding on to his job, but it was suitably irritating for his opposite number in Italy on Wednesday.

Cesc Fabregas saw his Como team race into a 4-0 lead over Man United in the third game of the league phase of the Champions League, and admitted that he was responsible for his team stepping back with a comfortable lead and allowing the visitors to get a futile goal back.

The 4-1 defeat in Lombardy means that United have won just two of their 11 matches in all competitions since Carrick was appointed on a permanent basis, against Sabah in the Champions League and against promoted Ipswich Town in the Premier League.

'It was my fault' Como eased up on Man United

Cesc Fabregas (Image credit: Alamy)

United conceded three times in the Ipswich match and were earned three points by a Bruno Fernandes hat-trick. The captain made his view on Carrick's tactics clear after the match. While Carrick moved to shift some of the blame back on his players, Fabregas assumed the blame for Como's change of attitude with the game already won.

Speaking after the match, Fabregas said: "I regret the last ten minutes because I was the one who told the team to slow down the tempo because there's another match and at 4-0 we could manage things a little.

"I told the players to stop running and stay in the middle and that is something I regret because I'm the first to always push them to fight until the end.

"Their goal hurt me because we dropped our intensity and dropped our defensive block, but it was my fault."
