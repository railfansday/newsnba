#1. Hospitals deliberately targeted by Sudan's RSF fighters, BBC investigation finds

Medics at el-Fasher's Saudi Hospital performed a Caesarean by phone light on the day it was bombed

A BBC investigation has found evidence that Sudanese paramilitary fighters systematically destroyed el-Fasher's healthcare system during their long siege and subsequent takeover of the city in Darfur a year ago.

UN human rights chief Volker TÃ¼rk called the findings "deeply disturbing", saying multiple attacks by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) on civilian infrastructure, including health facilities, were serious violations of international humanitarian law and may amount to war crimes.

The paramilitary group's health minister described the allegations as propaganda and a deliberate attempt by the national army and its allies to smear the RSF's reputation.

Using sources who were on the ground in el-Fasher, the BBC has found details of at least 43 separate attacks on hospitals and healthcare sites from May 2024 to October 2025 - some hit multiple times.

Under international law it is illegal to attack health facilities or their staff during conflict.

The investigation also reveals the toll on medical workers in the western city. In the period from April 2023 to October 2025, the BBC's data shows 120 were injured, 55 killed and 10 forcibly "disappeared" or abducted.

"The RSF [were] trying to evacuate the hospitals, to put them out of service because the health system is the cornerstone of any city or any state," says Dr Muhamed Suliman, a surgeon who was based in el-Fasher during the siege and spoke to the BBC.

"The RSF started hitting these areas so citizens have no longer any way to stay there. It was intense. It was deliberate. It was planned."

The investigation comes a year after the fall of el-Fasher, one of the most brutal chapters of the civil war which erupted in April 2023 between the RSF and Sudan's military.

Both sides have been accused of attacks on health facilities.

In March 2026, the World Health Organization (WHO) condemned the bombing of al-Daein hospital in RSF-held eastern Darfur, which killed dozens and forced the site to close. Sudan's military is believed to have carried out the drone strikes, although it has denied responsibility.

But events in el-Fasher represent "one of the clearest and most severe examples of the wider impact of the conflict on health care in Sudan," the WHO said in response to a BBC query.

UN investigators have reported that the fighting in el-Fasher bore the "hallmarks of genocide", where historical ethnic animosities have seen Arab militias - now the backbone of RSF forces - target non-Arab groups such as those that populate the city.

The BBC's investigation reveals for the first time a detailed chronology of attacks on healthcare during the long siege of the city.

Some medical facilities in el-Fasher closed down within weeks of the country's civil war starting in April 2023.

Crucially this included one of the main hospitals - the Teaching Hospital - located in a conflict zone in the middle of the city.

Its operations were moved to el-Fasher's South Hospital. There medical services continued, although increasingly hindered by power cuts and dwindling supplies.

The situation changed dramatically a year later, when the RSF mounted its siege of el-Fasher.

In late May and early June of 2024, the South Hospital was shelled several times as paramilitary fighters pushed forward from their positions in the east of the city.

The first bombing came as a shock, says Dr Suliman.

The area around the hospital that was hit was "very crowded because it was the only working hospital in the city providing surgical services", he says.

On that day medical staff operated on 16 injured patients, he added.

The attacks culminated in an RSF raid on 8 June. Hospital staff were taken by surprise - many of them escaped over the back wall as the RSF fighters entered the building, says Dr Mustafa Ahmed, a pharmaceutical specialist.

But the director general of the hospital, surgeon Izzeldin Ahmed Dawaud Asow, encountered a group of the paramilitaries outside his office.

They did not know he was a doctor, he said, but denounced him with ethnic slurs.

"There are no citizens here. You are Falangi," they said, using a derogatory term implying that non-Arab Sudanese are foreigners.

Dr Asow says he was beaten with rifle butts and lost consciousness at one point. Ultimately, he saved himself by pretending to be dead.

"They put their hand on my chest, and I stopped breathing. And they said: 'Leave him, leave him. The rubbish has died. Leave him.' They left me."

That same day the RSF was driven out by the Sudanese military and its allies and Dr Asow returned to find the bodies of several patients and the surgical equipment in his operating room thoroughly destroyed, he said.

The hospital closed and later fell into RSF hands when the army retreated.

Dr Alaa Eldin Nugud, a prominent Sudanese surgeon who has joined the RSF-backed civilian government as health minister, insisted the South Hospital was being used as a military base.

According to international law, hospitals can lose their protection if they are used by a party to the conflict to commit an "act harmful to the enemy".

In an interview with the BBC, Dr Nugud declared that missile launchers were set up on the grounds and shared a video showing remains of weapons filmed more than a year after the hospital closed, which he said supported his claim.

He also showed us what he called maps of landmines planted around the Teaching Hospital, which is located next to Sudanese military headquarters.

Doctors told us the hospital was not incorporated into the base and said they visited it to pick up medical equipment during the war.

The BBC has found no independent evidence of Dr Nugud's allegations. And the government of the Darfur region categorically rejected the charges.

After the South Hospital shut, its staff and remaining equipment were moved to the Saudi Maternity Hospital in the west of the city, where the army had its main bases.

Within days the pace of attacks on the facility picked up. At this stage the medical charity MÃ©decins Sans FrontiÃ¨res (MSF) sounded the alarm.

"We do not know if hospitals are being intentionally targeted, butâ¦ the belligerents are not taking any precautions to spare them," it said in a statement.

However, in the course of our investigation we spoke to six doctors who described a pattern of systematic targeting.

They told us how Saudi Hospital was repeatedly struck - on some days up to 25 times, with a mix of shelling and drones, the latter "intelligent rockets" with precision-guided attacks.

Smaller medical facilities also came under fire. The BBC's timeline records the steady closure of private hospitals and neighbourhood clinics.

Furthermore, when some of these healthcare buildings shifted locations, the alternate venues were also attacked.

The Specialised Children's Hospital, which shut down at the beginning of the war along with the Teaching Hospital, moved to two other locations, both of which were then shelled and forced to close.

Similarly, the main dialysis centre was struck and then attacked again after it relocated.

By the time the RSF seized the city in October 2025, the Saudi Hospital was the only functioning health facility left.

The medical staff there had rallied to try to keep working as conditions worsened.

Doctors described how sandbags were set up to protect the building.

They built underground bunkers out of steel containers and established a number of alternative sites to house and receive patients and conduct surgery as the threat of attacks and the damage to the main structure steadily increased.

Dr Asow said this makeshift surgery was attacked five times in 2025.

In one case, he says, he was operating on a patient's neck when the building was shaken by an explosion - the medical staff ran out in fear, but he stayed.

In another case, health workers had prepared the theatre for an operation, but he told them to move the patient elsewhere because it needed a deep clean given the relentless case load. Shortly afterward the room was destroyed by shells - and the patient survived.

"From that day, some of my staff called me 'Mr Deep Cleaning' because they think that the issue of deep cleaning saved that patient," he said.

Dr Asow also describes how drones tracked the movement of patients.

On 15 March 2025, people carrying the injured from the site of an attack to one of the temporary wards were followed by a hunting drone, he says.

It struck as they reached the gate of the building, killing nine people.

On 17 August of the same year a donkey cart was transferring patients to the alternative operating theatre because all the ambulances had been destroyed, Dr Asow told the BBC.

It was hit by a drone, killing two patients, the driver and the donkey, he says.

The RSF health minister dismissed these accounts as "a continuation of lies" by the military (SAF) and their local allies, known as the Joint Forces.

"Even the medics, and many of the people who were there, because of this conflict, these tribal issues, these racial issues, they are allied with the Joint Forces and with the SAF. That's why you don't depend on that narrative," he said.

Doctors told the BBC they believed RSF fighters deliberately targeted medical facilities because army or allied fighters were among their patients.

The onslaught of shelling and bombing culminated on 25 October 2025 with the RSF storming the hospital amid allegations of a massacre that killed more than 460 patients and their companions.

The RSF has denied a massacre occurred, although the WHO condemned the reported killings and satellite imagery appeared to show clusters of bodies on the blood-soaked grounds of the hospital.

The BBC shared details of its investigation with the UN human rights chief, who said our findings were "deeply disturbing", and consistent with the analysis of his office.

"Our monitoring and documentation shows the RSF repeatedly struck el-Fasher's main hospitals and makeshift health facilities, destroying or seriously damaging them and severely impacting access to health services for thousands of civilians trapped in the city," TÃ¼rk said.

Healthcare in el-Fasher is now being run by the RSF-backed government. It has released several videos showing the Saudi Hospital and the Teaching Hospital re-opened, cleaned up and seeing some patients.

Dr Nugud says South Hospital is also set to reopen. He denied that doctors were forcibly abducted, telling the BBC that instead they had chosen to stay on to work.

"I have a video in front of the children's hospital when we freed a consultant, a doctor and six of the medical staff who were working with the SAF, and then when el-Fasher got liberated, there was a forgiveness for all of them.

The BBC spoke to one of the doctors in the video, Dr Al-Haj Mohamed Ahmed. He told us he had no choice: "I was forced to be there.

"If I spoke out of line, they'd put a bullet through my head."

He was among several medical workers captured while trying to escape the city and sent back, along with Dr Mustafa Ahmed.

They distinguished themselves by bringing a cholera epidemic in a temporary prison at the paediatric hospital under control and were subsequently taken to Saudi Hospital.

"They started to say that you are the people of the city, and we take the city, so we need to work together for the sake of the city," says Dr Mustafa Ahmed, who describes waking up several mornings in a row at the prison to find 50 inmates dead of cholera.

A year on from the fall of el-Fasher, the trauma of this dark period in Sudan's civil war is still painfully sharp.

The front lines have shifted elsewhere with no signs of a resolution to the conflict. And attacks on healthcare continue as part of a wider and devastating humanitarian crisis.

#2. UK to draft in military aircraft to fight wildfires, Burnham says

RAF A400M planes will be able to fight wildfires if fire crews request them

Military planes and helicopters will be drafted in to tackle wildfires, Prime Minister Andy Burnham is to tell the UK's first summit on the issue.

Civilian fire services in all four nations will be able to request the support of the Royal Air Force's Chinook helicopters and A400M planes to fight wildfires on tough terrain such as remote moorland and steep hillsides.

Burnham said the measures were needed because "we cannot put people through another summer like this one".

The government will host its first "wildfire resilience summit" on Friday to discuss how to prevent, respond to and recover from such blazes after a record-breaking summer 2026.

There have been 1,350 wildfires in England and Wales this year, an all-time high and up by more than 300 on last year and more than 1,000 on 2024, according to figures from the National Fire Chiefs Council.

Records for the number of reported wildfires were also broken in 2022 and 2025 - but the UK's Climate Change Committee has warned the risk will rise in the coming decades.

Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said this was the first time the RAF's Chinooks and the four-engine A400Ms were being equipped to tackle wildfires. Some areas struggled with no air assistance in the summer.

Chinooks have practised picking up and dropping water from firefighting buckets in Portland harbour, UK

RAF Chinook crews have already helped respond, external to wildfires in Cyprus.

In addition to military aircraft, the government said it would also spend Â£45m on wildfire-specific personal protective equipment for firefighters on the ground.

It is lighter than current firefighting equipment which is designed for fires in buildings and not meant to be worn for a long time, the government added.

Ministers are also pledging a Â£25m scheme from April 2027 to April 2029 to restore dried-out peatlands in England so they are natural firebreaks, and financial support for farmers to fireproof their land such as by digging ponds.

"The government will also look at tougher local restrictions or product bans to stop people starting fires when the risk is high," a briefing ahead of the summit said, following a temporary ban on disposable BBQs.

Burnham will meet fire chiefs, local leaders and experts from tech companies at the summit.

"I stood in Stourbridge and watched families lose everything," he said on Thursday, recounting the night of a wildfire that destroyed 19 homes in the West Midlands in August.

"I will never forget it. Our firefighters were heroes that day, but heroes shouldn't have to fight with one hand tied behind their backs."

Earlier this month, MPs on the Environment, Food and Rural Affairs Committee said there were weaknesses in the UK's wildfire preparedness, including access to aerial firefighting resources, securing water supplies during major incidents and coordinating resources at a national level.

MPs said the current system for obtaining aerial firefighting support was "no longer fit for purpose".

At the summit, a "Wildfires AI Unit" will be announced to combine universities, tech firms and officials on the Cobra emergency committee, along with Met Office wildfire forecasts and closer work with devolved governments.

In August, the government convened a Cobra meeting before sending an emergency alert to millions of phones across England and Wales - which sparked a backlash from some.

Summer 2026 was the UK's hottest on record, according to the Met Office. In a study on how this summer was impacted by human-induced climate change, it said the heat experienced was made 130 times more likely.

"The wildfires that tore through our countryside this summer showed the urgent need to adapt to our changing climate," Environment Secretary Dame Angela Eagle said ahead of the summit.

"Nature is our greatest ally in preventing wildfires starting in the first place," she added.

Major incidents were declared for wildfires this year in areas such as Dunwich Heath and RSPB Minsmere in Suffolk, the Cairngorms, Hampshire and Holme Fen in Cambridgeshire.

#3. Grid operator says lights will stay on this winter after concerns

The body in charge of Britain's electricity network has given assurances that the lights will stay on this winter after concerns were raised by some of its engineers.

The National Energy System Operator (NESO) said the country's electricity system was prepared and "resilient to the challenges it may face" during the colder months.

However, its winter outlook report comes after an independent investigation found engineers had "high levels of stress and concern" about their ability to ensure adequate supply due to differences between renewable and gas-fired power generation.

NESO said supplies were expected to remain secure throughout the winter, though warned there could be "tight days".

It added that such days would result in it deploying notices to power producers that additional electricity generation may be needed. It said such an announcement was not a warning of power cuts and was routine.

"While we never take resilience for granted, households and businesses can be confident that Great Britain's electricity system remains secure, reliable and among the most resilient in the world," said Deborah Petterson, director of whole energy system resilience at NESO.

In a separate report, National Gas, a private company that owns and runs the country's gas network, and is separate from NESO, said it expected to have sufficient supply to meet demand this winter, but forecast gas for power generation to increase at peak demand due to the "intermittent" nature of renewables.

The release of the two reports comes as Britain's energy generation and security falls under increasing scrutiny, given the wars in the Middle East and eastern Europe have pushed prices up and sparked supply fears.

The country's electricity is generated from a wide range of sources, including renewables such as solar and wind, as well as more traditional gas-fired and nuclear power stations.

Wind power was the largest generator of electricity in Britain last year, accounting for almost 30% of all power, marginally above gas.

However, there appear to be challenges in the transition to renewables in ensuring supply keeps up with demand, according to findings from an investigation into practices at NESO, which was commissioned following allegations over operational decision making and record keeping at the grid operator.

Law firm Eversheds Sutherland, which carried out the independent investigation, said there were "high levels of stress and concern" within NESO's Electricity National Control Centre over the "current state of the energy industry generally" and the longer-term ability to keep it balanced.

It said this was, according to evidence gathered from staff, due to the fact that traditional methods of electricity generation such as gas were "easier to manage", compared to renewables, which were "less flexible", and were making the job of ensuring supply meets demand "more difficult".

The probe also found there to be a "significant disconnect" between those working in the control room and NESO's leadership.

Multiple witnesses expressed the view that those in senior leadership and executive positions "do not fully appreciate or understand either the way in which the control room works, or the reality of the pressures faced by those on the frontline who are responsible for 'keeping the lights on'", Eversheds' report said.

The investigation was prompted after Conservative shadow energy secretary Clare Coutinho said in July she had been contacted by a whistle-blower alleging that NESO had been "hiding information about how securely our grid is being run".

One of the claims was that control room staff were encouraged to conceal or alter records to avoid leaving a paper trail linked to efforts to stabilise the grid during a period of extreme heat in June.

This was done in order for NESO, which is owned by the government, to avoid public scrutiny or reputational damage, according to the allegation.

Eversheds said its investigation did not substantiate any of the allegations from whistle-blowers but did identify "cultural, governance and control issues" that it said were "significant findings in their own right".

Fintan Slye, chief executive of NESO, said the investigation identified "areas where we could improve how colleagues can raise concerns and how we respond to them".

"I want all colleagues at NESO to feel heard. We will act on the recommendations, working closely with our trade unions, and be open with colleagues about the progress we make," he added.

#4. Raye wins the Mercury Prize with This Music May Contain Hope

Raye is the 35th winner of the Mercury Prize, which was established to recognise albums that push the boundaries of music

Raye has won the 2026 Mercury Prize for her show-stopping second album This Music May Contain Hope.

A pop record sprinkled with golden age Hollywood glamour, it beat the likes of Sir Paul McCartney, Olivia Dean and Jade to scoop the Â£25,000 prize.

"I couldn't tell you how grateful I am to be holding this," said Raye, clutching her trophy at Newcastle's Utilita Arena. "I'm shaking, I'm jet-lagged, I'm tired. It's all blurry."

It is the first time a pop album has won the coveted prize - which recognises the best British or Irish album of the last 12 months - since M People's Elegant Slumming in 1994.

Speaking to the BBC afterwards, Raye said she'd "poured everything I had" into the record, which features the global hit song Where Is My Husband?

"Some of these songs are seven minutes. They're indulgent. They're maximalist."

Across 17 tracks and 73 minutes, the record tells a cinematic story about a relationship that ends in tears on the rainy streets of Paris, then follows our plucky heroine as she dusts off the debris and emerges stronger and more resilient.

Along the way, it features both of her grandparents, the soul legend Al Green, four separate orchestras and more than 100 musicians.

Released in March, the album went straight to the top of the UK charts and won glowing reviews from critics.

Rolling Stone magazine praised its "old-school show-tune razzle-dazzle [and] big-band swing frills", while the NME raved that, "Raye's agile voice has never sounded so good, and her pen has never been so strong".

In the BBC's own review, it was described as an "eccentric, kitsch and dramatic" record where Raye "unapologetically goes for broke".

Raye comes out swinging on her new album, This Music May Contain Hope

But the album almost never happened. Two years ago this week, the singer's car was stolen, along with all of her songwriting notebooks and demos.

Although they were eventually recovered, it delayed work on This Music May Contain Hope by almost a year.

Looking back, she was philosophical about the delay.

"I was distraught. I was very worried, but you know, it all turned out great.

"Every little slip, every little accident, every little thing, it creates the path that you take.

"And it's easy to spend time wishing things were the other way. But right now, I certainly do not."

The star performed the big band number I Hate The Way I Look Today during the Mercury Prize ceremony

Established in 1992, the Mercury Prize was envisaged as an antidote to the commercially-focused Brit Awards, recognising albums that moved music forwards, without recourse to fashion or trends.

Of the last 35 winners, 20 have been debut albums - from artists including Arctic Monkeys, Suede and Franz Ferdinand.

Many people have mistakenly assumed it is a prize for first albums - but this year's shortlist included only two: Dove Ellis's heartfelt and delicate Blizzard, and Jade's That's Showbiz, Baby.

'I'm speechless': Olivia Dean on making UK chart history

In the run-up to this year's ceremony, Olivia Dean had been the bookmakers' favourite for her second album, The Art of Loving.

An easy-going, soulful collection of love songs, it had already been named album of the year at the Brit Awards and the Mobos, and is widely expected to earn the singer a Grammy nomination in the same category next month.

Speaking backstage, the singer said she was overwhelmed by the success.

"I don't think I've processed it yet, to be honest," she said. "I feel like I'm still in this crazy whirlwind of it all.

"And maybe, when I take a break over Christmas, I'll reflect on the sofa."

Other nominees included the rapper Kojey Radical, Geordie jazz act Knats, and former Little Mix star Jade - who was the first ever X Factor winner to receive a Mercury nomination.

The shortlist also included the charity compilation album Help (2), recorded in aid of Warchild, which featured new music and cover versions by artists such as Olivia Rodrigo, Damon Albarn, Depeche Mode and The Last Dinner Party.

The tracklist also included nine former Mercury Prize winners: Arctic Monkeys, Young Fathers, Wolf Alice, Pulp, Sampha, Portishead's Beth Gibbons, Arlo Parks, English Teacher and Ezra Collective.

Last year, the prize was won by Newcastle singer-songwriter Sam Fender, who donated his Â£25,000 winnings to grassroots music venues, helping to support up-and-coming songwriters around the UK.

#5. Amber warning in place as Storm Austen hits parts of UK

An amber weather warning has come into force in parts of the UK as Storm Austen brings a swathe of powerful winds and heavy rain on Friday.

South West England is forecast to be worst hit by the storm which the Met Office warns could bring travel disruption, power cuts and a possible danger to life.

Wind gusts of 60-70mph, with 80mph in exposed locations, are forecast across Cornwall and parts of Devon, where the Met Office warning is in force until 10:00 BST.

Wind gust of 45-60mph are forecast more widely across most of England and Wales, where two less severe yellow warnings will be in force throughout Friday. A separate yellow warning will also cover part of Scotland, including Glasgow and Edinburgh, from 06:00 BST.

Winds of this strength will blow some trees over, bringing a significant risk of transport disruption and there may also be some power cuts.

Winds of this strength will blow some trees over while buildings could also potentially be damaged, leading to a risk of flying debris at the height of the storm.

A number of schools are closed in the impacted areas with many others opening later than usual to avoid the strongest winds.

Train cancellations and speed restrictions are possible because of the increased danger of branches and trees blocking railways.

The company alluded to problems the company experienced during the Covid-19 pandemic, when Zuckerberg began working outside of the office and mostly from his large compound in Kauai, Hawaii.
